FairTrial x AI Hackathon · Track 2: Observation to Legal Evaluation

Ratio

Reads a trial’s monitoring record and gives the reviewing lawyer findings to check line by line, each linked to the sentence it rests on. The legal evaluation stays with the lawyer.

Offline by design

Ratio runs offline, on the reviewer’s own computer. Everything runs on one laptop, built only on open-source software, and nothing is sent to another computer.

This page only describes Ratio. It runs no scripts and sets no cookies: no analytics, forms or external resources. Like any website, its host (Vercel) keeps standard request logs.

Ratio runs four checks against the international fair-trial standards (ICCPR Articles 9 and 14). Every finding links to the exact sentence it rests on: click it and the source document opens with that passage highlighted.

The problem

TrialWatch lawyers review large volumes of monitoring notes, transcripts and court documents, and the hackathon’s Track 2 brief calls that review a bottleneck. Before judging whether a trial was fair, a lawyer has to reconstruct what happened from that record. Three things make the review slow and inconsistent.

  • Violations hide in omissions

    A missing interpreter or late access to counsel never appears as a sentence in the notes. A reviewer has to notice what isn’t there.

  • Delay is arithmetic buried in prose

    Deciding whether detention or trial delay was excessive means pulling dates from scattered entries and comparing them by hand.

  • There is no memory across cases

    Each case is reviewed on its own, so patterns by the same judge or court are hard to see.

What Ratio does

Ratio reads a case’s monitoring notes, indictment and judgment, and runs four checks against ICCPR Articles 9 and 14; the fourth also looks across cases, at rulings coded by people. The name comes from ratio decidendi, the reasoning behind a decision: Ratio’s central question is whether the court did its own reasoning.

  1. 1Absence Detector Which guarantees of ICCPR Art. 14(3)(a)–(g) have no evidence of compliance?
  2. 2Procedural Clock Were detention and trial delays excessive?
  3. 3Reasoning Reuse Detector Did the court reason independently of the prosecution?
  4. 4Judicial History Tracker Do a judge’s rulings across monitored cases show a pattern that warrants review?

Module 1

Absence Detector

Which guarantees of ICCPR Art. 14(3)(a)–(g) have no evidence of compliance?

What you see
A rights coverage grid. Where the notes say nothing, Ratio gives a follow-up question for the monitor instead of a finding.
In the demo case
No note ever mentions an interpreter. Ratio does not invent a finding. It gives the monitor a follow-up question, and next to it a note it does not count as evidence: the defendant’s first language is Ostric.

Module 2

Procedural Clock

Were detention and trial delays excessive?

What you see
A timeline with measured intervals, red only where the one confirmed benchmark (48 hours to see a judge) is exceeded.
In the demo case
Dates are pulled from three documents and the interval is computed in code: 4 to 6 days from arrest to the first appearance before a judge (Ratio reads dates to the day, not the hour, so the range covers any time of day). General Comment 35, paragraph 33, treats 48 hours as ordinarily sufficient. The appearance before the prosecutor the day after the arrest does not stop the clock: a prosecutor is not a judge. The other benchmarks are measured, cited and marked “needs legal review”, never coloured.

Module 3

Reasoning Reuse Detector

Did the court reason independently of the prosecution?

What you see
The judgment beside the indictment, with copied and paraphrased passages highlighted, the share of the court’s reasoning traceable to the indictment, and defence arguments the judgment never answers.
In the demo case
60% of the court’s own reasoning can be traced to the indictment: 48% word for word and 12% as a close paraphrase. Legitimate quotation doesn’t count: the recited charge, the parties’ arguments and the quoted statute are greyed out, each tagged with the reason, and excluded before scoring. One defence argument, about how the phone messages were extracted, gets no response in the judgment.

Module 4

Judicial History Tracker

Do a judge’s rulings across monitored cases show a pattern that warrants review?

What you see
A judge profile: rates with sample size and confidence intervals, compared with other judges of the same court and charge type.
In the demo case
Detention at the first appearance is 9 of 9 cases against 4 of 10 for the other judges of the same court and charge type. It is a “pattern that warrants review”, never a verdict, and n and the intervals are always shown. Two indicators are not distinguishable from the baseline, and two are hidden because they have fewer than 5 cases. A name written with initials only waits for a person to confirm it.

On every page

Every finding opens its source

What you see
Any evidence button opens the source viewer: the document, the line and the exact characters, checked character for character against the text Ratio read.
Why it matters
A finding without a source is dropped, so a reviewer can check every finding against the record before relying on it.

How it works

A case folder becomes one case record in which every item carries its source. The four checks read only that record, never the raw text.

  1. Case folder

    The monitoring notes, the indictment and the judgment (.txt, .md or .pdf), listed in a case.yaml, with an optional rulings.yaml of coded rulings.

  2. Extraction

    Code splits the text into sentences and passages with character offsets. The local model returns dated events and party arguments as exact quotes. Each quote must be found in the source text; anything not found is dropped and counted.

  3. Dates

    Dates are parsed in code. If the model and the parser disagree, both are kept and the event is marked for review.

  4. Case record

    Every item carries its source span: document id, start, end and exact text. The record is stored in SQLite.

  5. Four checks and a provenance check

    Each check reads only the case record, so it can be built and tested on its own. Each span is re-read from its document, and a finding whose span does not match is dropped.

  6. Review

    A Streamlit app on 127.0.0.1, which accepts connections only from the same computer, and an evaluation script.

What the model never does

The model never does arithmetic, never grades a person, and never decides a status on its own:

  • Intervals are computed in code.
  • The model’s labels count only when they name the rubric indicator they rely on.
  • The judge statistics come from rulings coded by people.

The default local model is qwen2.5:7b-instruct, run by Ollama on the same computer. The demo replays its recorded answers, so it works with Ollama stopped and Wi-Fi off.

Responsible AI safeguards

Five hard rules. Each is enforced in code and checked by tests.

  1. 1

    Runs offline, on the reviewer’s own computer

    No cloud services, no third-party APIs, no telemetry.

    How Ratio enforces it. The model client only talks to Ollama on 127.0.0.1 (the same computer) and refuses cloud-hosted models. A network guard blocks every other connection in the app, the command line, the evaluation and the tests. Hugging Face runs in offline mode, and the embedding model loads from models/. Streamlit runs with usage statistics off, on 127.0.0.1 only, with no error-page links and no web fonts or remote themes. The app refuses to start if any of these settings is not in effect.

    How it is tested. The full pipeline runs under the network guard. A global Streamlit config that tries to override the settings is ignored. The browser checks found every request going to 127.0.0.1: in the demo run measured on 8 October 2026, the browser made 364 requests, all to 127.0.0.1. python -m ratio preflight checks these settings, any STREAMLIT_* environment variables that would override them, and whether the running Ollama server (or its settings file) has its cloud features disabled.

  2. 2

    Provenance on everything

    Every finding points to the exact text it rests on.

    How Ratio enforces it. The model returns exact quotes, and Ratio locates each one in the source text; quotes it can’t find are dropped. A finding must carry at least one source span (document id, start, end, exact text). Before anything is stored or shown, every span is re-read from its document, and a finding whose span does not match is dropped.

    How it is tested. The evaluation reports provenance before and after this check and fails on any unsourced finding. App tests check that every piece of evidence on every page has a button that opens its source.

  3. 3

    No verdicts about people

    Ratio describes what the record shows and leaves conclusions to the reviewing lawyer.

    How Ratio enforces it. The messages of findings and the labels come from fixed templates (ratio/config/messages.yaml). Model notes appear only under “Model note (unverified)”, after words that characterise a person are removed. Judge indicators say “pattern that warrants review” only when the confidence interval for the difference from the baseline excludes zero, with the significance level divided across the indicators shown. Otherwise they say the judge is not distinguishable from the baseline at this sample size. Every rate shows n and a 95% Wilson interval. Indicators are hidden below 5 cases. The baseline is the same court and charge type only. Rulings are coded by people, never by the model. A name counts for a judge only at the same court, and doubtful names wait for a person to decide.

    How it is tested. Known-value tests for the Wilson and Newcombe intervals. App tests check the judge page for blocked terms and for the fixed note, n and intervals.

  4. 4

    Public or synthetic data only

    Synthetic material is marked SYNTHETIC, in every file and on every page.

    How Ratio enforces it. Every synthetic text file starts with a SYNTHETIC: line, and every YAML and JSON file declares synthetic: true. Public material must cite where it was published. Before the app analyses a case folder uploaded to it, the user must confirm that the documents are synthetic or already public. A SYNTHETIC banner appears on every page and in the source viewer.

    How it is tested. Every demo data file is checked for the marker. App tests check the banner on every page.

  5. 5

    No invented legal thresholds

    Only a confirmed benchmark can be marked as exceeded.

    How Ratio enforces it. Only one benchmark is confirmed: General Comment 35’s 48 hours to bring a detainee before a judge (para. 33). Every other benchmark has a citation and no threshold, is marked “needs legal review”, and is measured but never coloured. Only confirmed benchmarks can be marked as exceeded.

    How it is tested. Config tests check that only the 48-hour benchmark is confirmed and that an unconfirmed benchmark is never coloured.

Limitations

In brief. The full list is in the README.

  • The evaluation is in-sample, on one synthetic case. Ratio has not been tested on published TrialWatch reports yet.
  • Legal review is pending. The rubric indicators, the three unconfirmed benchmarks, the reuse standards and the ruling codes were drafted by the engineering team and need review by a lawyer. Until then they are marked “needs legal review”, and only the 48-hour benchmark is applied.
  • English only. Multilingual input was cut from the hackathon scope.
  • A small local model makes mistakes. Every model output is checked against the source text, and dates are parsed in code, but an event the model misses is simply absent. Reading a whole case live takes minutes.
  • Reuse counting is conservative. A judgment sentence that quotes the law and then applies it is excluded as a statute quotation, so any copying in it is not counted.
  • The Judicial History Tracker runs on synthetic history only. Coded rulings must be written by hand, and TrialWatch’s case selection makes the rates unrepresentative, as every judge page says.
  • Tested on macOS with Apple Silicon. The locked setup targets macOS; on Linux it is untested, and PyTorch no longer publishes wheels for Intel Macs.
  • Not built yet: the detention-renewal mode, a report draft export, the steelman-the-state agent, the jurisprudence linker, and a feedback loop from lawyers’ corrections (stretch goals in the team’s hackathon proposal).

Run it locally

You need a Mac with Apple Silicon, Python 3.11, about 8 GB of free disk space, and 16 GB of memory for the live model. Do the setup once, while online. After that, Ratio needs no network.

  1. Follow the setup in the README: clone the repository, install the pinned dependencies, download the embedding model (about 90 MB) and the local language model through Ollama (about 4.7 GB), and turn off Ollama’s cloud features.
  2. Check that the computer is ready with python -m ratio preflight.
  3. Start the app with streamlit run app/main.py and open http://127.0.0.1:8501. The app only accepts connections from that computer.
  4. On the Case page, click Load the demo case. It replays the model’s recorded answers, takes about 4 seconds, and works with Ollama stopped and Wi-Fi off.

To present it, follow the three-minute demo script, which has fallback screenshots.

Data and standards

The demo case

Republic of Calderra v. Daro Venn is entirely synthetic: the country, court, people, minority language and laws are invented. It has four hearing notes, the indictment and the judgment, with planted issues:

  • no interpreter is ever mentioned, though the defendant’s first language is a minority language;
  • the defendant is arrested on 14 February and first appears before a judge on 19 February (4 to 6 days, since Ratio reads dates to the day), after an earlier appearance before a prosecutor that does not stop the clock;
  • four judgment passages are copied from the indictment and one is lightly paraphrased;
  • one defence argument is never answered.
Judicial history
20 synthetic cases generated from seed.yaml: 19 from the demo case’s court and one from another invented court.
Standards
The ICCPR, and the Human Rights Committee’s General Comment No. 32 (Article 14) and General Comment No. 35 (Article 9). Paragraph numbers were checked against the official UN documents.
Your own cases
Public or synthetic material only. Before the app analyses a case folder you upload to it, you confirm that the documents are synthetic or already public and contain no confidential monitoring material. The folder stays on your computer. A case folder has a case.yaml listing its documents (.txt, .md or .pdf), with the type, court and charge type, and whether the material is synthetic or public (with the source).

No real monitoring notes and no published TrialWatch reports are in the repository.
